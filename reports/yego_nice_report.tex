\begin{abstract}
\noindent YEGO publishes the position and battery level of its scooters in real time through the
General Bikeshare Feed Specification (GBFS). I collected this feed every two minutes in Nice and
Barcelona from Sunday 11 October at 18:23 to Monday 12 October at 08:32 (426 snapshots for Nice) and computed operational
indicators with a dbt pipeline. Three findings stand out for Nice: (i) YEGO disables every scooter
below 15\% battery; (ii) at the evening demand peak (19h), 16 scooters were waiting for a battery swap, while 50 of the 57 swaps I detected happened between 20h and 02h, that is, after the peak; (iii) about 11.2\% of the fleet is disabled
for reasons other than battery.
\end{abstract}

\section{Data and method}

\textbf{Data.} For every scooter that is parked and not rented, the feed \cite{gbfs} gives its
position, battery level, and two flags, \texttt{is\_reserved} and \texttt{is\_disabled}. A scooter
being ridden is not listed. A small Python collector stores each snapshot as a Parquet file; over the
14 hours studied here it had no interruption. The feed refreshes positions about every four
minutes, which is the real time resolution of the study.

\textbf{Anonymised identifiers.} The \texttt{bike\_id} field is regenerated at every request: two
snapshots four minutes apart shared no identifier, although 113 scooters had not moved at all. To
follow scooters anyway, I match the scooters of two consecutive snapshots. A pair $(i,j)$ is a
candidate if both have the same type and are less than 15\,m apart, with cost
\begin{equation}
  c_{ij} = d_{ij} + \lambda\,\lvert b_i - b_j \rvert, \qquad \lambda = 0.5\ \text{m per battery point},
\end{equation}
where $d_{ij}$ is the distance and $b$ the battery level. I keep the one-to-one matching with the
lowest total cost (Hungarian algorithm \cite{kuhn1955}), plus a second pass for GPS jumps up to
150\,m when the battery is unchanged. A simple nearest-neighbour rule, my first attempt, broke about
10\% of the tracks when scooters are parked side by side. With the assignment, 99.7\% of the
observations are linked to a scooter of the previous snapshot.

\textbf{Events and quality checks.} A \emph{departure} is a scooter that disappears from the feed
(rented, or picked up by the staff). A \emph{battery swap} is a rise of at least 20 battery points on
the same scooter, kept only if it was disabled just before or did not move. Zones are cells of about
500\,m named with OpenStreetMap \cite{nominatim}. Everything is modelled with dbt on DuckDB
\cite{duckdb}, with data tests on missing values, positions outside the service area, gaps in the
collection and the battery rule (16 passed, 1 warning, 0
failed). The warning is a real finding: one Barcelona scooter is published at latitude 0,
longitude 0 in every snapshot.

\section{Results}

\textbf{Fleet availability.} On average 234 scooters were listed in Nice and 84\%
were available (Figure~\ref{fig:fleet}). 14.7\% were disabled: 3.5\% because of
their battery and 11.2\% for another reason. No enabled scooter ever had less than
15\% battery, so YEGO disables scooters under this threshold. The ``other reason'' group has a
stable base of about 22 scooters and rises to 35 around 21h,
during the evening operations. Barcelona, with about 1,734 scooters, follows the same 15\% rule and has very similar shares: 82\% available, 6\% disabled for battery and 11\% for another reason.

\textbf{Battery swaps and demand.} I detected 57 battery swaps in Nice. In 53 of them the scooter was disabled just before, and the battery went from 21\% to 91\% on average. Figure~\ref{fig:timing} puts demand and battery operations on the same time axis. The busiest hour was 19h with 58 departures. At that moment 16 scooters were disabled because of their battery. Swaps were concentrated between 20h and 02h (50 of 57), and the queue only reached its minimum at 02h. In other words, the swap round worked well, but it started after the moment when the scooters were most needed. The morning peak looks different: at 07h there were 41 departures while only 3 scooters were waiting for a battery, because the queue had been cleared during the night.

\section{Recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Start the battery swap round before the evening peak.} At 19h, the busiest hour of the period (58 departures), 16 scooters (7\% of the listed fleet) could not be rented because of their battery. Most swaps were done between 20h and 02h. Starting the round about two hours earlier would put these scooters back on the street when demand is highest. This should be checked over several evenings before changing the schedule.
  \item \textbf{Move about 12 scooters from low-demand to high-demand zones.} Vernier, Vieux Nice and Carré d'Or had 44 departures for only 4.9 available scooters on average, while Carabacel, La Costière and Valrose kept 15.3 scooters for 1 departure (Table~\ref{tab:zones}). Night-time, when demand is lowest, is the natural moment for this. The exact number should be calibrated on several weeks of data.
  \item \textbf{Review the scooters disabled for reasons other than battery.} At the last snapshot, 23 scooters were disabled with a correct battery, and 20 of them did not move for at least 6 hours. This group is 11\% of the fleet on average and it is not reduced by battery swaps. Checking why they are disabled (breakdown, maintenance, vehicle to collect) could free part of this capacity.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and next steps}

The study covers one evening and one night, so these patterns must be confirmed over several weeks
(the collector is still running). A departure can be a rental or a pick-up by the staff, rides shorter
than four minutes are invisible, and the tracking can still make mistakes in dense parking spots.
With YEGO's internal data, I would first join departures with real rentals to measure true demand,
then measure the delay between a scooter being disabled under 15\% and its return to service,
and finally test the recommendations on a few zones against similar zones left unchanged.
